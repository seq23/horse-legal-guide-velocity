# What Horse Owners and Equine Businesses Commonly Miss About Intellectual Property & Brand

## Direct answer

Using a barn name first does not register it, and registering it does not make it yours everywhere. Those are two different systems, and most equestrian brand problems come from treating them as one.

In the United States, trademark rights begin with commercial use. If you have been running Ridgeline Sporthorses for nine years, you already hold common-law rights in the geographic area where people actually know that name. Federal registration is a separate, optional step that adds nationwide constructive notice, a public record, and a much stronger position if someone else adopts a confusingly similar name later.

The practical consequence: the barn that used the name first and never filed can still lose the ability to expand, and the barn that filed without checking who was already using the name can still lose a dispute. Neither of those outcomes is decided by who paid a filing fee.

## Citation data atom: USPTO trademark filing fees

The U.S. Patent and Trademark Office publishes its fee schedule, and the numbers are specific enough to plan around. These are the amounts on the USPTO fee schedule as revised 14 August 2026, for fee amounts effective 19 January 2025.

| Line item | Fee | When it applies |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Base application, filed electronically through Trademark Center | $350.00 per class | Every application. "Per class" means per category of goods or services, so lessons and apparel are two classes, not one. |
| Insufficient-information surcharge | $100.00 per class | Charged when the application does not supply the information the USPTO requires at filing. |
| Free-form identification surcharge | $200.00 per class | Charged for each additional group of 1,000 characters beyond the first 1,000 when you write your own description of goods and services instead of selecting from the Trademark ID Manual. |
| Paper filing, base application | $850.00 per class | Paper is no longer accepted except in limited circumstances. |

Two things in that table are worth reading twice.

The first is "per class." A training operation that also sells branded saddle pads and also runs a summer camp is looking at three classes, and the base fee multiplies accordingly. People routinely budget $350 for a filing that is actually a $1,050 filing before a single surcharge lands.

The second is the $200 free-form surcharge. It exists to push filers toward the pre-approved Trademark ID Manual descriptions. Equestrian services do not always map cleanly onto those pre-approved entries, which is exactly the situation where a filer starts writing custom language and stacks characters without realising each additional 1,000 costs another $200 per class.

What this table cannot tell you is the total cost of getting a registration, because that depends on whether the examining attorney issues an office action, whether anyone opposes, and whether you use counsel. There is no published USPTO figure for "average total cost to registration," and any number presented as one is an estimate rather than a fee. This page does not offer one.

## Document checklist: before you file

Work through these in order. The order matters, because each step can make the next one unnecessary.

1. Write down the exact mark. Not the logo, not the tagline, not the barn's Instagram handle — the specific word or design you want protected. If you cannot write it on one line, you have more than one mark.
2. Write down every category of goods and services you actually sell today. Lessons, boarding, training, sales commissions, clinics, apparel, feed, breeding services. Each is potentially its own class at $350.
3. Search the USPTO database for identical and similar marks in those classes. Similar is the standard, not identical: a phonetic match or a near-synonym in the same class is a real obstacle.
4. Search outside the register. Google, state business registries, breed association member directories, and social platforms. Common-law users do not appear in the USPTO database, and they can still block you.
5. Confirm you are actually using the mark in commerce for each class, or that you have a genuine intent to. Filing for classes you do not serve invites problems later.
6. Decide who owns the mark before filing. The individual trainer or the LLC. Changing this later means an assignment, not an edit.
7. Check the free-form character count against the Trademark ID Manual before submitting, so the $200-per-1,000-character surcharge is a choice rather than a surprise.
8. Diary the maintenance dates the moment a registration issues. Registrations lapse for missed filings far more often than they are lost in disputes.

## What people often miss

The thing people miss is that the horse world runs on names that are already shared.

A farm name that includes a common geographic feature, a discipline word, and the word "equestrian" is a name that dozens of operations have independently arrived at. Distinctiveness is what trademark law protects, and highly descriptive names sit at the weak end of that spectrum. "Memphis Dressage Training" describes a service in a place; it does not distinguish one provider from another. A name like that can be very hard to register and very hard to enforce even when you have been using it for a decade.

The second thing people miss is that the horse's name and the business's name are governed by different systems entirely. Breed registries maintain their own naming rules and their own databases. A registered name with a breed association is not a trademark. It does not stop a competitor from opening a barn under that name, and it does not create rights you can assert against them.

The third is sponsorship. A rider who accepts product, cash, or entry support has often signed something that grants a company the right to use the rider's name, likeness, and results in marketing. Those grants are frequently broader than the rider understood, and they frequently outlast the sponsorship term. If you are building a personal brand while riding under someone else's, read the licence grant and the term separately.

## Where the risk actually sits

Brand disputes in this industry rarely start with a lawsuit. They start with a name collision that becomes visible at exactly the wrong moment — a new website, a rebrand, an expansion into a neighbouring state, a show series that draws two similarly named operations to the same venue.

At that point the question is not who is right in the abstract. It is who has the better documented position: earlier evidence of continuous commercial use, a clearer geographic footprint, a registration, or none of those. Barns that kept invoices, show programmes, advertisements, and dated marketing material have something to point at. Barns that have only a memory of when they started do not.

That is why the record-keeping habit matters more than the filing decision for many small operations. A registration is valuable. Provable first use is what makes it defensible, and what sometimes makes the registration unnecessary.

## When registration is genuinely worth the money

Not every operation needs a federal registration. The cases where the $350-per-class arithmetic tends to pay for itself:

- You sell goods, not only local services. Apparel, tack, feed, and supplements travel; lesson programmes usually do not.
- You are planning to expand beyond your current market, or to franchise or licence the name.
- The name is genuinely distinctive rather than descriptive, so a registration would actually be enforceable.
- You are building something you intend to sell. A registered mark is a transferable asset that shows up on a balance sheet; goodwill attached to an unregistered descriptive name is much harder to convey.
- You have already had one near-miss with a similarly named operation.

If none of those apply, the honest answer may be that your money is better spent on a clean set of service contracts and a documented use file. Wise Covington sees both situations, and the difference between them is usually a business question before it is a legal one.
