# What Horse Owners and Equine Businesses Commonly Miss About Therapeutic Riding & HIPAA

## Direct answer

Holding sensitive information about a rider does not make a programme subject to HIPAA. HIPAA reaches covered entities and their business associates, and most therapeutic riding centres are neither.

The federal definition is narrow and specific. Under 45 CFR 160.103, a covered entity is one of exactly three things: "A health plan," "A health care clearinghouse," or "A health care provider who transmits any health information in electronic form in connection with a transaction covered by this subchapter."

Read the third one closely, because it is the only one most equine programmes could plausibly fall into, and it has two conditions stacked together. You must be a health care provider, *and* you must transmit health information electronically in connection with a covered transaction — the standard electronic transactions such as claims and eligibility checks. A programme that is not a health care provider does not qualify. A provider that never submits electronic covered transactions does not qualify either.

The consequence catches people off guard in both directions. Many centres that have been running HIPAA compliance programmes for years were never covered entities. And many that assumed they were exempt are covered, because a therapist on staff bills insurance electronically.

## Decision tree: which situation are you in

| Programme shape | Health care provider? | Electronic covered transactions? | Covered entity under 45 CFR 160.103? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Recreational therapeutic riding, private pay only, no clinician on staff | No | No | No |
| Adaptive riding, private pay, PATH-style instructor but no licensed clinician delivering treatment | No | No | No |
| Hippotherapy delivered by a licensed PT, OT, or SLP who bills insurance electronically | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Equine-assisted psychotherapy by a licensed mental health clinician billing insurance electronically | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Same licensed clinician, private pay only, no electronic covered transactions | Yes | No | Generally no |
| Barn that rents arena space and horses to an outside clinician who bills insurance | No | No | No, but see the business associate question below |
| Barn performing a function for a covered entity involving protected health information | No | Not applicable | No, but may be a business associate |

The final two rows are where most equine facilities actually sit, and they are the rows that get analysed incorrectly.

## The business associate trap

Not being a covered entity is not the end of the analysis. The same regulation defines a business associate, and the definition begins: "Except as provided in paragraph (4) of this definition, business associate means, with respect to a covered entity, a person who:" — the paragraphs that follow describe performing functions or services for a covered entity that involve protected health information.

For an equine facility, this matters when the relationship with a clinician is more than a landlord relationship. If a covered clinician rents your arena, brings her own records, and you never see them, you are a landlord. If your staff schedule her clients, take intake information, keep her session notes in your office, or handle her billing paperwork, the relationship has changed shape and the business associate question is live.

Facilities frequently drift across that line without noticing. It usually starts with a scheduling favour.

## What people often miss

The largest misconception is that HIPAA is a general privacy law. It is not. It is a rule that attaches to specific entities performing specific functions. Information about a rider's diagnosis is sensitive whether or not HIPAA applies, but the obligations that attach to it come from different places depending on who holds it.

The second misconception runs the other way: that if HIPAA does not apply, nothing does. That is wrong, and it is the more dangerous error. A programme outside HIPAA can still face state privacy statutes, professional licensing board obligations for its clinicians, contractual confidentiality duties, obligations attached to grant funding, and ordinary negligence exposure for mishandling sensitive records. Concluding "we are not a covered entity" answers one federal question and none of the others.

Third, volunteers. Therapeutic riding runs on volunteers, and volunteers hear things — a diagnosis mentioned at the mounting block, a medication discussed with a parent, a behaviour explained so a sidewalker can respond correctly. Whatever the federal analysis says, a programme that has never told its volunteers what not to repeat has a real exposure that no regulation defines for it.

Fourth, photographs. Programmes fundraise on images of riders, and riders in therapeutic programmes are frequently minors or adults with disabilities. Consent for a photograph is a separate document from consent for treatment, and a general media release signed at intake is often written far too broadly to cover a specific campaign use years later.

## A practical sequence for a facility

1. Write down every clinical or quasi-clinical service delivered on your property, and who delivers each one.
2. For each, record whether the person is a licensed health care provider.
3. For each licensed provider, ask one question: do they submit electronic covered transactions, such as insurance claims or eligibility checks? Ask them directly and get the answer in writing.
4. For anyone answering yes, treat that provider as a covered entity and work out whether your facility performs any function for them involving protected health information.
5. Where it does, address the business associate relationship in writing rather than by habit.
6. Where your facility is neither a covered entity nor a business associate, do not build a HIPAA programme. Build a records and confidentiality policy sized to the risks you actually have.
7. Separate your files physically and digitally. Clinical records held for a clinician should not sit in the same drawer or the same shared drive folder as boarding invoices.
8. Write a volunteer confidentiality rule in plain language, one page, and have every volunteer sign it each season.
9. Separate photo and media consent from treatment and participation consent, and state what uses are covered and for how long.
10. Confirm what your state requires, because state privacy law can be stricter than the federal floor and is not preempted where it is.
11. Review the analysis whenever a new clinician starts working on the property. The answer changes with the roster, not with the calendar.

## What this costs

There is no published fee associated with determining HIPAA status, because it is an analysis rather than a filing. There is no registration, no certificate, and no government charge for concluding that you are or are not a covered entity.

Beware of the certification market that has grown around this. "HIPAA certified" is not a status the federal government confers on an organisation, and a paid certificate does not establish compliance. Any figure you are quoted in this space is a vendor's price, not a regulatory cost, and this page does not endorse or estimate one.

## Where this leaves a programme

Most therapeutic riding centres will finish this analysis outside HIPAA and still needing a real confidentiality practice. That is a perfectly coherent place to land, and it is a better place than a centre that bought a compliance binder it did not need while leaving its volunteer conversations and its photo consents unaddressed.

The centres that need the most care are the ones whose answer changes — a private-pay programme that adds an insurance-billing clinician has moved, and usually nobody redoes the analysis.
