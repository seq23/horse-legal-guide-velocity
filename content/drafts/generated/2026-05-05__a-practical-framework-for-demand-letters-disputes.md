# A Practical Framework for Demand Letters & Disputes

## Direct answer

A demand letter has no legal force by itself. It is one person's written account of a dispute and a request that you do something about it. Ignoring it is not a violation of anything.

That is also why ignoring it is usually the wrong move. The letter is the last stage at which the dispute is cheap, private, and reversible. Once it becomes a filing, it becomes public, slower, and considerably more expensive, and everything you said or failed to say in the meantime becomes part of the record.

So the useful question is never "do I have to respond." It is "what does responding, or not responding, do to my position." Those are different questions and only the second one has a real answer.

## The first 48 hours

Before you decide anything about the merits, do these. In this order, and before you reply to anyone.

1. Photograph the envelope, including the postmark and any tracking label, before you throw it away. The date of receipt can matter and the envelope is the only proof of it.
2. Note any deadline the letter states, and note that a deadline invented by the sender is not a legal deadline. Write both facts down.
3. Stop deleting. Texts, emails, DMs, voicemails, photos, videos, invoices, vet records, farrier records, show entries, transport receipts. Preservation starts the moment a dispute is foreseeable, not when a case is filed.
4. Export the message threads rather than screenshotting them. Screenshots lose timestamps and metadata that a full export keeps.
5. Write your own timeline of events from memory today, while it is fresh, and date it. Do not send it to anyone.
6. Do not call the sender to sort it out. A friendly phone call in a horse dispute becomes a disputed account of what was admitted.
7. Do not post about it. Not in a barn group, not on a discipline forum, not vaguely.
8. Check whether you have insurance that might cover this. Many policies require prompt notice, and late notice is its own problem separate from the underlying claim.
9. Find the paperwork the dispute is actually about: the bill of sale, the boarding agreement, the lease, the training contract, the text where terms were agreed.
10. Only then decide whether and how to respond.

Steps 3 and 4 are the ones people skip and the ones that most often cause irreversible damage.

## Comparison table: how the letter was sent, and what that proves

Senders choose a delivery method to create proof. The methods and their published prices, from the USPS Notice 123 price list effective 12 July 2026:

| Method | USPS price | What it proves | What it does not prove |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| First-Class Mail alone | Postage only | Nothing about delivery | That it arrived, or when |
| Certified Mail | $5.55 per item, plus postage | That the item was mailed and a delivery record exists | What was inside the envelope |
| Certified Mail with Return Receipt, hard copy (PS Form 3811) | $5.55 plus $4.65, plus postage | Delivery plus a physical card bearing the recipient's signature | What was inside the envelope |
| Certified Mail with Return Receipt Electronic | $5.55 plus $2.91, plus postage | Delivery plus an electronic copy of the signature | What was inside the envelope |
| Email or text | $0 | Sending, and usually opening | Nothing about the recipient's agreement |

Two useful conclusions come out of that table.

First, the entire proof apparatus around a demand letter costs a sender under eleven dollars. Receiving something by certified mail tells you the sender wanted a record. It tells you nothing whatsoever about whether the claim is good.

Second, and more usefully for you: none of these methods proves contents. A signed green card establishes that an envelope was delivered, not that it contained the letter now being described. This cuts both ways, and it is why the photograph of the envelope in step 1 matters.

## What people often miss

People miss that the response is evidence.

A demand letter is frequently drafted to provoke a reply, because the reply is where people explain themselves. A defensive, detailed, emotionally written answer that walks through the whole history is a gift to the other side: it commits you to a version of events before you have reviewed your own records, and it often concedes facts the sender could not otherwise prove.

The second thing people miss is the deadline illusion. "You have ten days to respond" is a negotiating device. Real deadlines come from statutes of limitations, from contractual notice provisions, and from court rules — and those run on their own clocks, some of which may already be running and none of which the sender controls. The ten days is not real; the limitations period is, and it is the one nobody looks up.

The third is that horse disputes are unusually document-poor and unusually emotion-rich. Six-figure sales close on a handshake and a text. Boarding relationships run for years on an agreement nobody can find. When the dispute arrives, both sides genuinely remember different deals, and both are telling the truth as they recall it. The side that can produce a contemporaneous record wins arguments that feelings cannot settle.

The fourth is barn-community pressure. Equine disputes play out in a small world where both parties show at the same venues and share a farrier, a vet, and a trainer. That pressure produces two bad instincts: settle immediately to make it stop, or escalate publicly to defend a reputation. Both decide the matter on social terms rather than on the documents.

## Reading the letter properly

Separate it into four things, on paper:

- **The factual claims.** What the sender says happened. Mark each as agreed, disputed, or unknown.
- **The legal theory.** What they say that entitles them to. Often unstated, and its absence is informative.
- **The demand.** The specific money or action requested, and by when.
- **The threat.** What they say they will do next. Note whether it is a described consequence or something else — a threat to report you to a licensing body or a breed association, or to publicise the dispute, is a different kind of statement and worth flagging.

A letter heavy on threat and light on legal theory is usually a weaker letter than it feels while you are reading it. A short, specific, unemotional letter that cites the clause it relies on is usually the stronger one, even though it reads as less aggressive.

## What resolution actually costs

There is no published figure for the cost of resolving an equine dispute, and this page will not offer one. Costs vary by forum, by whether experts are needed to establish soundness or value, by state, and by how much documentary work has to be reconstructed after the fact.

What can be said without inventing a number: the reconstruction is the expensive part. Disputes where the paperwork exists and can be produced in an afternoon cost a fraction of disputes where the first month is spent establishing what the deal was. That difference is decided years earlier, at the point somebody did or did not write the agreement down. It is the reason Wise Covington would rather see the boarding agreement in January than the demand letter in July.

Small claims courts exist in every state with their own monetary limits and their own simplified procedure, and for smaller equine disputes they are often the proportionate forum. Those limits are set state by state and are not listed here; check your own state court's published limit rather than any national figure.
