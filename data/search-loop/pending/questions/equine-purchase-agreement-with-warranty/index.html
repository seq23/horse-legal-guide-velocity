Understanding Equine Purchase Agreements with Warranties

An equine purchase agreement with a warranty is a written contract where the seller makes specific promises regarding the horse’s condition, health, or performance. Unlike an 'as-is' sale, these agreements define the seller’s liability if the horse fails to meet agreed-upon standards, often requiring precise language to be legally enforceable.

Published 12 November 2026 · Wise Covington PLLC

What is an express warranty in a horse sale?

An express warranty occurs when a seller makes a specific affirmation of fact or promise about the horse that becomes part of the basis of the bargain. This often relates to the horse’s health, temperament, or ability to perform a specific task. In many jurisdictions, these warranties are governed by the Uniform Commercial Code (UCC), which applies to the sale of goods. Whether a horse is considered a 'good' under the UCC often depends on state law and the specific circumstances of the transaction.

When a buyer and seller include a warranty in their agreement, they are essentially shifting the risk of certain defects from the buyer to the seller. If the horse does not meet the criteria outlined in the warranty, the contract should specify the buyer’s remedies. These might include a return of the horse for a refund, a price adjustment, or other agreed-upon resolutions. Without clear, written terms, enforcing a verbal promise can be difficult, as courts often look to the written document to determine the intent of the parties.

How does an 'as-is' clause affect a warranty?

Many horse sales are conducted on an 'as-is' basis, which is a legal term intended to disclaim implied warranties. By signing an 'as-is' agreement, the buyer typically accepts the horse in its current condition, including any unknown defects. This language is meant to protect the seller from future claims regarding the horse’s health or performance. However, the effectiveness of an 'as-is' clause often depends on whether the seller made fraudulent misrepresentations prior to the sale.

It is important to distinguish between implied warranties and express warranties. While an 'as-is' clause may successfully disclaim implied warranties, such as the warranty of merchantability, it may not override an express warranty written into the contract. If a seller provides a written guarantee in the contract, that specific promise may take precedence over a general 'as-is' disclaimer. Buyers and sellers often review these clauses carefully to ensure the document accurately reflects their mutual understanding of the horse's condition at the time of transfer.

What role does the pre-purchase exam play?

A pre-purchase exam is a common step in the horse-buying process, but it is not the same as a warranty. The exam provides the buyer with a professional opinion from a veterinarian regarding the horse's physical condition at a specific moment. While the findings of this exam often inform the terms of the purchase agreement, the exam itself does not guarantee the horse’s future health or performance.

When a contract includes a warranty, the parties may reference the pre-purchase exam as a baseline for the horse's condition. If the warranty covers specific issues, the exam results might be attached as an exhibit to the agreement. This helps define the scope of the seller’s promises. If the horse later develops a condition that was not present or identifiable during the exam, the warranty language will determine whether the seller is responsible. This is why many parties choose to clearly define the duration and scope of any warranty provided in the final contract.

Drafting and reviewing the purchase agreement

Drafting a purchase agreement requires attention to detail, as the language used determines the rights and obligations of both parties. A well-drafted contract typically includes the identities of the parties, a full description of the horse, the purchase price, and the specific terms of any warranties. If the parties intend for a warranty to exist, it should be stated clearly in writing rather than relying on verbal statements made during the negotiation process.

Parties should also consider how the agreement interacts with other documents, such as a bill of sale or a deposit agreement. Frequently asked questions

Are horses considered goods under the law?

In many U.S. jurisdictions, horses are classified as goods under the Uniform Commercial Code. This classification means that sales transactions are often subject to commercial law principles, including those related to warranties, contract formation, and the rights of buyers and sellers when disputes arise regarding the condition of the animal.

What is the difference between soundness and health?

Soundness typically refers to a horse's physical ability to perform a specific task without lameness or pain, while health refers to the horse's general medical condition. A horse may be healthy but not sound for a specific discipline, or sound for light work but not for high-level competition.

Do private horse sellers have the same warranty obligations as commercial dealers?

Generally, the law treats private sellers and commercial dealers differently. Commercial dealers are often held to higher standards regarding implied warranties of merchantability. Private sellers may have more flexibility to disclaim warranties, though they remain bound by the specific terms of any written contract they sign with the buyer.

Can a warranty be added to a bill of sale?

A bill of sale is primarily a document that transfers ownership. While it can contain warranties, it is often better to use a comprehensive purchase agreement to detail complex terms. Including specific warranty language in a formal contract helps ensure that all conditions and remedies are clearly defined for both parties.
